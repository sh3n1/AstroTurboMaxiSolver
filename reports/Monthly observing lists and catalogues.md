# Compute the months, ask before shipping lists

AstroFinder's safest and most useful next step is not another curated list but a **computed "by month" view**: assign every object the app already knows to a month from its RA/Dec, the user's location and the real start of darkness, which needs no third-party selection and is correct for both hemispheres and for daylight saving time. For new named lists, the strongest candidates are the **ASSA Top-100** (100 southern binocular objects, 92% NGC/IC, no stated terms), **RASC Deep-Sky Gems** (154 objects already split by season), **RASC Explore the Universe** (a binocular beginner list almost fully covered by existing data), the **Astronomical League Urban program** (100 objects for light-polluted skies, which matches the Valencia design case) and, as a public-domain-leaning monthly seed, **NASA Hubble's Night Sky Challenge** (about 9 Messier/Caldwell objects per month, per hemisphere). None of the society lists comes with a licence that permits reuse in software: the Astronomical League, RASC, BAA and SAC sites all say "All rights reserved", and curated "best N" selections are exactly what compilation copyright (US) and the database right (EU) can protect. That puts the four lists already shipped, Herschel 400/II, SAC 110 and RASC Finest NGC, in the same grey zone as the new ones, so a short permission email to each club is the main licensing action. Book selections (O'Meara, Sue French, Hartung) and SkyMaps.com material should not be shipped at all without a publisher licence. Data needs beyond OpenNGC are small and predictable (a few dozen Melotte, Collinder, Barnard, Palomar, UGC and named objects), and NASA HEASARC tables are the cleanest bulk source. **This is not legal advice**, and several facts below are marked unverified because the underlying pages could not be fetched.

Two southern historical lists sit just behind these. The **Bennett catalogue** has 152 objects compiled by the South African comet hunter Jack Bennett with a 5-inch refractor, published in 1969 and updated in 1974, including Omega Centauri, 47 Tuc and the Tarantula; the AL runs it as a program at 107 and 152 objects ([ASSA Bennett](https://assa.saao.ac.za/?p=2885); [AL Bennett](https://www.astroleague.org/?p=7224)). The **Dunlop 100** is Glen Cozens and Graeme White's selection of the best objects from James Dunlop's 1826 Parramatta catalogue of 629 objects, published in Sky & Telescope in June 2001 and ordered by Dunlop number ([Dunlop 100 PDF](https://prescottastronomyclub.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Dunlop-100.pdf)). ASSA's deep-sky marathon page also offers a 41-object Lacaille (1755) list ([ASSA marathons](https://assa.saao.ac.za/?p=8827)). Bennett and Lacaille are themselves historical catalogues, so shipping them whole involves less of somebody else's selection; the Dunlop 100 is a magazine selection and carries more risk than the full Dunlop catalogue would.

The Astronomical League has the largest menu, **63 programs by its own 2022 count** and "70-plus" per Astronomy magazine, with top levels "often 100" objects and lists "designed to take you through the different seasons" ([RASC Handbook 2022 AL article](https://rasc.ca/sites/default/files/Astronomical-League_Observing_Programs.pdf)). Confirmed details exist for only a few. **Bright Nebula** has 150 nebulae for an 8-inch or larger scope with UHC/OIII filters, for both hemispheres, and is the one program with an official **XLSX** list ([AL Bright Nebula](https://www.astroleague.org/bright-nebula-observing-program)). Stellar Evolution has 100 objects and Two in the View 109 groups, according to SkySafari's community repository ([SkySafari repository](https://go.skysafariastronomy.com/repositories/index.php?dir=skylist%2FAstronomical+League&theme=onyx)). The Carbon Star manual is sold rather than posted free ([AL Carbon Star](https://www.astroleague.org/carbon-star-observing-club)). The sizes of Binocular Messier (about 102), Deep Sky Binocular (about 60, by season), Double Star (100), Open Cluster (about 125), Globular Cluster (50), Planetary Nebula (about 110), Carbon Star (about 100) and the Southern Sky programs are **unverified**: they come from the researchers' prior knowledge, because the D to Z program pages were not fetched.

Several well-known lists fit AstroFinder poorly. The **RASC Deep-Sky Challenge** (45 objects by Alan Dyer and Alister Ling, including Palomar 11, Jones 1, the Horsehead and quasars) asks for a 25–30 cm scope and nebula filters ([RASC DSC](https://www.rasc.ca/node/47252)). The AL's Arp, Flat Galaxy, Galaxy Groups and Local Group programs are faint large-aperture lists. The RASC lunar programs (135 features in the Isabel Williamson program) and the AL Lunar, Comet, Asteroid and Galileo programs have no fixed sky positions and would need a Moon view rather than the finder ([RASC IWLOP](https://rasc.ca/node/47179)). The BAA's Deep Sky Section runs programmes by object class (Messier, Caldwell, planetary nebulae, globulars) rather than a distinct curated list ([BAA programmes](https://britastro.org/section_information_/deep-sky-section-overview/observing-programmes)). SAC also publishes a "Best 110 Double Stars" and its own **Urban List** for Metro Phoenix, which fits the city-sky design case and deserves a look; their contents and terms were not checked ([SAC Urban List](https://www.saguaroastro.org/the-urban-list)). No RASNZ or Stellafane list was found.

## Every curated list, including the shipped ones, lacks a reuse licence

The core legal point is the same for every candidate. In the US, *Feist v. Rural* (1991) leaves facts such as designations unprotected but lets an original **selection or arrangement** be protected, with an "extremely modest" creativity bar ([IT Law Wiki](https://itlaw.fandom.com/wiki/Copyright_protection_of_databases)). In the EU, where the developer is based, Directive 96/9/EC gives a separate 15-year **database right** against extracting all or a substantial part of a database, independent of copyright ([Database right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database_right)). A "best 110" or "154 gems" list is a hand-made selection, and an app ships the whole list, so "it's only designations" is a weak defence. Small lists make the investment argument weaker, but no court case on astronomy observing lists was found. **This is a risk analysis, not legal advice.**

Against that background, the publishers' own terms are what matters, and none grants software reuse. Every Astronomical League page fetched shows "© All Rights Reserved" with no separate terms ([AL Observing Programs](https://www.astroleague.org/observing-programs/)). The League's style guide for manual authors asks them to assign copyright to the League and uses the notice "No part of this publication may be reproduced or utilized in any form or by any means without written permission", with an exception only for personal use; it also says an email can suffice as permission ([AL Style Manual](https://www.astroleague.org/files/u220/AL%20White%20Paper%20-%20Observing%20Manual%20Style%20Manual.pdf), seen as a search summary). Every RASC page and PDF says "All rights reserved", and the observing pages name the Observing Committee as the contact ([RASC Observing](https://www.rasc.ca/observing)). The BAA also reserves all rights ([BAA programmes](https://britastro.org/section_information_/deep-sky-section-overview/observing-programmes)). ASSA's Top-100 and marathon pages state no terms at all, which is not a licence but makes a permission request straightforward ([ASSA Top-100](https://assa.saao.ac.za/?p=1105)). SkySafari, the market leader, ships only generic "best of" lists, and users import AL lists themselves as community `.skylist` files; no commercial app was found shipping an AL list under a stated arrangement ([SkySafari help](https://support.simulationcurriculum.com/hc/en-us/articles/115015943028-Observing-Lists-Help)).

That applies equally to what AstroFinder already ships. The **Herschel 400** (Ancient City Astronomy Club) and **Herschel II** (Rose City Astronomers) are selections run as AL programs; **RASC Finest NGC** comes from an "All rights reserved" RASC checklist; and the **SAC 110 Best NGC** comes from a club that sells it as a 124-page book ([SAC 110 book](https://www.saguaroastro.org/book-sacs-110-best-of-the-ngc)). The code comments in `src/app/core/herschel.ts`, `sac-110.ts` and `rasc-finest-ngc.ts` and the Settings credits treat the numbers as facts with credit given, which is a reasonable position for designations but not a permission. Two existing data points need a separate check. SAC's older deep-sky database documentation said "released for private use" and "Please do not sell this database in any form", and no formal licence was found for version 8.1 ([SAC 5.0 doc](https://discmaster.textfiles.com/view/11575/FishMarket%20v1.0.iso/fishies/201-225/disk_219/deepsky/sac50.zoo/sacdoc.txt); [SAC downloads](https://www.saguaroastro.org/sac-downloads/)). And d3-celestial's DSO JSON is derived from SAC 8.1 with no separate data licence, even though its code is BSD-3-Clause ([d3-celestial](https://github.com/ofrohn/d3-celestial)). Settings credits the Messier data as coming "via d3-celestial"; whether that Messier file shares the SAC-derived terms was **not verified** and should be checked.

The risk ladder that follows from the evidence runs from lowest to highest. Whole historical catalogues (Messier, Lacaille, Dunlop's full list, Bennett as a catalogue) involve little modern selection. Club lists with no stated terms (ASSA Top-100) need a request that is likely to be granted. Club lists marked "All rights reserved" (AL, RASC, SAC, BAA, including the four already shipped) need written permission and credit. **Book and magazine selections need a publisher licence and should not ship without one**: O'Meara's *Hidden Treasures* (109 objects, "This publication is in copyright", Cambridge University Press), his *Southern Gems* (120), Sue French's *Deep-Sky Wonders* (100 tours by season and month from her S&T columns) and the S&T Dunlop 100 ([CUP front matter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/37040/frontmatter/9780521837040_frontmatter.htm); [Deep-Sky Wonders](https://peribo.com.au/product/deep-sky-wonders/)). Using a club's program name, such as "Deep-Sky Gems" or "Astronomical League Urban", also implies affiliation, so permission should cover the name, or the list should be renamed neutrally. When permission is refused or slow, there are two fallbacks: let users import a list they downloaded themselves (CSV or `.skylist`), so the app ships no third-party selection, or ship the app's own themed lists ("City targets", "Binocular doubles") with an independent selection.

## Monthly guides are prose; NASA's challenge is the only clean list

Month-by-month guides are plentiful but almost all are editorial prose about planets, the Moon and meteor showers. NASA/JPL's "What's Up", running monthly since 2007, is event-driven, for example the Orionid peak and the Moon near the Pleiades in October 2026, and is not a fixed deep-sky list ([JPL What's Up Oct 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/videos/whats-up-october-2026-skywatching-tips-from-nasa/)). The Royal Observatory Greenwich sells an annual month-by-month book co-authored by Dominic Ford, whose In-The-Sky.org content is © Dominic Ford ([RMG shop](https://shop.rmg.co.uk/collections/books/products/2026-guide-to-the-night-sky-a-month-by-month-guide-to-exploring-the-skies-above-britain-and-ireland); [In-The-Sky about](https://in-the-sky.org/about.php)); the full In-The-Sky terms were blocked and are unverified. ASSA's *Sky Guide Africa South* is a commercial annual handbook ([ASSA Sky Guide](https://assa.saao.ac.za/publications/sky-guide/)). **SkyMaps.com's Evening Sky Map must not be used**: its terms forbid electronic distribution and any commercial use without written, paid permission ([SkyMaps terms](https://skymaps.com/terms.html)). Sky & Telescope's permissions pages returned HTTP 403 and should be treated as all rights reserved.

The **NASA Hubble Night Sky Challenge** stands out. Each month has a northern and a southern list of Messier and Caldwell objects that Hubble has imaged, rated by difficulty from 1 to 3, with a featured object per month (January M1, June M83, November M33, December C67) ([Challenge](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubbles-night-sky-challenge/)). NASA media are generally not under US copyright, provided NASA is credited, no endorsement is implied and the NASA insignia is not used ([NASA media guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)). The catch is that the challenge is coordinated with the Astronomical League, and the League's rights in the selection are **not stated**. Every object is already in the app's Messier and Caldwell data, so it costs nothing in data terms. The one permissively licensed computed monthly list found is deepskycorner.ch (CC BY 4.0), but it has no bulk download and defaults to the equator ([Deep Sky Corner](https://deepskycorner.ch/objects.en.php/month/1)).

## The best monthly structure is computed for the user's sky

Printed guides place an object in the month when it is well placed in the evening, roughly on the meridian around 21–22 h, and the meridian RA at a fixed clock time moves about **2 h per month**. Spain's IGN atlas makes the same point: next month's chart is this month's sky two hours later ([IGN SkyAtlas](https://www.ign.es/web/resources/acercaDe/libDigPub/SkyAtlas2023_eng.pdf); [PhysicsForums](https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/how-can-i-estimate-local-sidereal-time-in-my-head.636728)). The rule of thumb breaks down in three ways that an app can fix. The research derived (unverified, to confirm in code) that at 21:00 local mean time in mid-month the meridian RA is about (2 h × month + 1.5 h), and that a rule like "2 h × (month − 3)" gives roughly the Sun's RA, the sky behind the Sun, not the evening sky. Valencia's clock time runs about one hour ahead of local mean time in winter and two in summer, which shifts the evening meridian by 1–2 h of RA. And at 40–50°N in June, astronomical darkness starts around 23:00–00:30, so a fixed 21:00 is still twilight. Computed sites use stricter rules, such as at least 5° altitude during astronomical night for a set latitude ([Deep Sky Corner](https://deepskycorner.ch/objects.en.php/month/1)), but 5° is far too lenient for a city sky; the Sculptor Galaxy (C65) culminates at only about 25° from Valencia.

How observers actually use lists points to the structure. Printed handbooks split their lists by season, advanced lists run in RA order, and Astronomy magazine recommends **4–6 targets per evening** and spreading the 110 Messier objects over a year ([Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/backyard-observing-lists/)). NASA lists each month's objects easiest first and per hemisphere. SkySafari's "Tonight's Best" uses dusk-to-dawn darkness for the current place, and users complain that it shows objects not visible in the evening ([SkySafari search help](https://support.simulationcurriculum.com/hc/en-us/articles/115015867647-Search-Help)). No survey data on preferences was found, so this ordering rests on magazine advice and forum posts.

The research therefore recommends computing rather than copying. An object belongs to month M if, on the 15th at the user's location, it is at least about 30° high (about 20° for bright objects) and within about ±2 h of the meridian, at the start of astronomical darkness (Sun at −18°) or 21:00 clock time, whichever is later. A simpler variant gives each object the one month in which it transits nearest the start of darkness. Above about 48.5°N in midsummer there is no astronomical darkness, so the rule needs a nautical-twilight fallback. Seasons follow as three-month groups, with names flipped in the southern hemisphere, which stays compatible with the RASC Finest NGC season tabs. In the UI, "Tonight" is the default, "By month" has 12 tabs with the current month selected, each month shows about 10 objects sorted easiest first by default with "show all", and RA or constellation order is available for planning a route. A **Messier marathon** mode around the March/April new moon falls out of the same machinery, since the Sun sits in a Messier-free gap near RA 21h40m–23h20m and the route runs in RA order ([Universe Today](https://universetoday.com/100472/why-this-weekend-is-perfect-for-a-messier-marathon/amp)). All of this uses only astronomy-engine and the positions the app already has, and adapts to latitude automatically, which no printed guide can do.

## Extra object data is a small, mostly public-domain set

Most candidate lists are 85–100% Messier/NGC/IC, so OpenNGC already resolves them. The extras are small and predictable. They include Melotte 20, 25 and 111, Collinder 399, Kemble's Cascade and Bayer-named doubles plus Mira and Algol (EtU); UGC 5373, UGC 6253, Arp 321, Tombaugh 5, the double quasar and three Levy asterisms (Deep-Sky Gems); Palomar 11, Jones 1, Barnard 33 and 72 (Deep-Sky Challenge); Trumpler 10 and Bernes 157 (Dunlop 100); and 8 non-NGC/IC objects in the ASSA Top-100 ([EtU](https://rasc.ca/sites/default/files/EtUworkbookComplete%2002%2013%202020%20for%20web.pdf); [DSG](https://www.rasc.ca/sites/default/files/Deep-Sky_Gems_Final_221107.pdf); [Dunlop 100](https://prescottastronomyclub.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Dunlop-100.pdf)). These fit the hand-made `src/app/core/extra-targets.ts` the branch already has. The Levy asterisms have no external catalogue entry, so their positions would come from the RASC list itself, one more reason to have RASC permission. Double-star lists (RASC 110, AL Double Star, SAC Best 110 Doubles, Urban's star list) need a WDS source keyed by discoverer code (STF, STT) that goes beyond "bright doubles", and the Urban, Carbon Star and Variable Star lists need GCVS or HD/HIP-named variable and carbon star positions.

The OpenNGC addendum cannot fill these gaps: it holds only **64 objects** (33 ESO, 6 Melotte, a few Harvard, Hickson, UGC and PGC, Barnard 33 and Collinder 399) and no Sharpless entries, and anything taken from it stays CC-BY-SA in its own file ([OpenNGC addendum](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mattiaverga/OpenNGC/master/database_files/addendum.csv)). For bulk catalogues, **NASA HEASARC copies are the cleanest route**. The Harris globular cluster catalogue (2010 edition, Palomar globulars included) is published on data.gov under "U.S. Government Works", with a citation requested ([data.gov Harris](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/milky-way-globular-clusters-catalog-december-2010-version)), and HEASARC hosts Lynds' Bright Nebulae with NGC/IC/Sharpless cross-references, which OpenNGC itself uses for nebula sizes ([HEASARC LBN](https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/W3Browse/all/lbn.html)). CDS/VizieR is not a blanket open licence: its rules say access is free "essentially for scientific use" and that commercial use depends on each dataset's origin ([CDS rules](https://cds.u-strasbg.fr/vizier-org/licences_vizier.html), seen as a snippet only). Wikidata is CC0 and suits common names and cross-identifications such as "Sh2-155 = Cave Nebula" ([Wikidata licensing](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Licensing)). Stellarium's DSO catalogue (over 94,000 objects) sits in a GPL-2+ package with no separate data licence and must be avoided ([Debian copyright](https://sources.debian.org/src/stellarium/24.3-1/debian/copyright)). Gaia-based open-cluster catalogues such as Cantat-Gaudin 2020 (about 2,000 clusters) need checking: if they inherit Gaia's licence, believed to be CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO (unverified), they would be copyleft ([GAVO record](https://dc.g-vo.org/rr/q/lp/custom/CDS.VizieR/J/A+A/640/A1)).

Whether HEASARC hosts Barnard, LDN, Sharpless, vdB, Arp, Hickson, Abell, UGC, Collinder or the Dias open-cluster table was **not confirmed**: only `lbn` and `globclust` were seen. The researchers' object counts for those catalogues (Barnard 349, LDN 1,802, Sh2 313, vdB 158, Arp 338, Collinder 471, Melotte 245) are background knowledge and should be checked on VizieR before writing import scripts. The planned UI should also handle objects with no magnitude, since dark nebulae have only an opacity class and LBN and Sharpless only brightness classes.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. The most valuable "monthly list" for amateur astronomers is not one anyone publishes: it is a list computed for the user's latitude, clock and city sky, and AstroFinder already has every piece needed to build it. That also removes the licensing problem for monthly structure, because a rule ("best placed after dark, at least 30° high") produces no third-party selection. Curated named lists remain worth having for the motivation of a certificate-style checklist, but every one of them, the four already shipped included, depends on goodwill from a club rather than a licence. One batch of short permission emails to the AL, RASC, SAC and ASSA would clear the existing lists and unlock the best new ones at the same time.

The open questions are narrow. The AL's D to Z program contents, the full AL Urban list, the ASSA Top-100's 8 non-NGC entries, HEASARC's coverage of the remaining old catalogues, the Gaia data licence and the terms behind the d3-celestial Messier file all need checking before code is written. A lawyer, not this report, should judge whether shipping designation-only lists without permission is acceptable in the EU.
