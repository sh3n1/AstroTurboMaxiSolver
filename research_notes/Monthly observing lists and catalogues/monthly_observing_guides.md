# Month-by-month and season-by-season observing guides, and how an app could offer a "by month" structure

Research date: 2026-11-11. Not legal advice. Several sites (in-the-sky.org, cambridge.org, skyandtelescope.org) blocked automated fetching (bot protection or HTTP 403), so their terms are only partly verified. Those cases are marked below.

## Which public month-by-month guides exist, what they contain, and their licences

### Takeaway
Monthly guides are common but almost all are editorial prose: planets, the Moon, meteor showers and a few deep-sky highlights. Only a few publish a clean per-month list of designations. The best-structured free one is NASA/Hubble's "Night Sky Challenge", with about 9 Messier and Caldwell objects per month, separate northern and southern lists, and difficulty ratings 1 to 3. Commercial and society guides (Sky & Telescope, Astronomy, RMG book, SkyMaps.com, ASSA) are copyrighted. SkyMaps.com explicitly forbids electronic, published or commercial use without written permission.

### Cited Findings

**NASA / JPL "What's Up" (monthly video and transcript)**
- NASA's longest-running web video series, which started in April 2007. It is produced monthly at JPL, with transcripts and sky charts on science.nasa.gov/skywatching/whats-up. — [JPL What's Up Nov 2024](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/videos/whats-up-november-2024); [NASA What's Up June 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/skywatching/whats-up-june-2026-skywatching-tips-from-nasa/)
- Content is event-driven: planets, Moon conjunctions and meteor showers. For example, October 2026 covered the Orionid peak on 21–22 Oct and the Moon near the Pleiades on 27–28 Oct. It is not a fixed deep-sky list. — [JPL What's Up Oct 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/videos/whats-up-october-2026-skywatching-tips-from-nasa/)

**NASA Hubble "Night Sky Challenge" (with The Astronomical League)**
- Each month has two lists of recommended objects, one for the Northern Hemisphere and one for the Southern. Targets are Messier (M) or Caldwell (C) objects that Hubble has imaged. It is coordinated with The Astronomical League, which recognises observers who complete it. — [NASA Hubble's Night Sky Challenge](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubbles-night-sky-challenge/)
- Featured object per month: Jan M1, Feb C39, Mar C48, Apr C53, May C21, Jun M83, Jul M107, Aug M16, Sep M27, Oct C22, Nov M33, Dec C67. — [NASA Hubble's Night Sky Challenge](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubbles-night-sky-challenge/)
- Example, November:
  - Northern list: M31 (1), C14 (1), M32 (2), C23 (2), C56 (2), C65 (2), M33 (3), M74 (3), M110 (3).
  - Southern list: the same, except it replaces C14 with C104.
  - Difficulty runs from 1 (easiest) to 3. The page gives no time of night or latitude criteria beyond "visible in November".
  - [NASA Hubble Night Sky Challenge – November](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubbles-night-sky-challenge-november)
- It recommends a dark sky and a telescope of at least 6 inches. Binoculars and small scopes are enough for the brighter targets. — [NASA Hubble's Night Sky Challenge](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubbles-night-sky-challenge/)
- NASA licensing in general:
  - NASA media are generally not subject to US copyright, and NASA should be credited as the source.
  - Commercial use must not imply NASA endorsement.
  - The NASA insignia and logotype are not public domain.
  - Third-party material NASA uses with permission keeps its owner's copyright.
  - [NASA images and media usage guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)

**Southern hemisphere**

  - Local clubs republish monthly "deep sky object of the month" notes based on it, for example Hermanus Astronomy Skynotes.
  - ASSA also posts Deep-Sky Bulletins.
  - [ASSA Sky Guide](https://assa.saao.ac.za/publications/sky-guide/); [Hermanus Skynotes Nov 2025](https://www.hermanusastronomy.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Skynotes-November-2025.pdf)

**Messier marathon**
- Done around the March/April new moon. The Sun sits in a Messier-free gap at about RA 21h40m–23h20m, so from some northern latitudes all 109–110 objects can be seen in one night. Observers go in increasing RA, from M74/M77 setting in the west to M30 rising before dawn. — [Universe Today](https://universetoday.com/100472/why-this-weekend-is-perfect-for-a-messier-marathon/amp); [Astronomy.com marathon](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/get-ready-to-run-this-years-messier-marathon/)

### Inferences
- The Messier, Caldwell and NGC designations themselves are facts and can be used freely. The month-to-object assignment in a specific guide is a selection that may be protected as a compilation, particularly under the EU database right. That protection is weak for a short list of about 9 items, but the safe course is not to copy a third-party selection wholesale. Not legal advice.
- The NASA Hubble monthly lists are the only well-structured, monthly, two-hemisphere designation list found that is likely free to reuse.
  - NASA material is generally not copyrighted. The co-produced Astronomical League component is uncertain.
  - The app could credit "Inspired by NASA Hubble Night Sky Challenge" without implying endorsement and without using the NASA logo.
  - Even so, computing its own assignment is cleaner and adapts to the user's latitude.
- SkyMaps.com TESM must not be used: its terms explicitly bar electronic distribution and commercial use.
- In-The-Sky.org, S&T, Astronomy, RMG, Star Walk and ASSA should be treated as all-rights-reserved. Use them as inspiration for structure only.

## How guides decide which month an object belongs to (method, latitude, twilight, DST), and what amateurs find most useful

### Takeaway
Guides assign objects to the month in which they are well placed in the evening, roughly crossing the meridian at about 21–22 h local time. The meridian RA at a fixed clock time advances about 2 h per month, because sidereal time gains about 4 min per day. Computed sites use stricter criteria: altitude of at least 5° during full astronomical darkness, for the user's latitude. Amateurs use month or season lists to choose what to observe and constellation or RA order to observe efficiently. Short lists of about 4–10 objects work best.

### Cited Findings
- Local sidereal time (LST) equals the right ascension currently on the local meridian. — [Acadia: Sidereal time](https://plato.acadiau.ca/courses/phys/astro/lessons/siderial.html); [Sheffield PHY105 sidereal](https://vikdhillon.staff.shef.ac.uk/teaching/phy105/celsphere/phy105_sidereal.html)
- Rule of thumb: "Sidereal Time = local time + (2 hours × months since Sept. 22)", with LST approximately equal to local (solar) time at the September equinox. — [PhysicsForums thread](https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/how-can-i-estimate-local-sidereal-time-in-my-head.636728)
- The exact GMST/GAST formula from the Julian date is given by USNO. Apparent and mean sidereal time differ by about 1 s at most. — [USNO GAST](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/GAST)
- Next month's chart is the same sky two hours later. — [IGN SkyAtlas 2023](https://www.ign.es/web/resources/acercaDe/libDigPub/SkyAtlas2023_eng.pdf)
- Computed monthly criteria: "at least 5° above the horizon" during "the night (end to begin astronomical twilight)", for a configurable latitude. — [Deep Sky Corner](https://deepskycorner.ch/objects.en.php/month/1)
- Season-based lists dominate printed handbooks: the RASC Observer's Handbook divides its Messier and NGC lists by season. Lists for advanced observers often run in increasing RA. — [Astronomy.com – Backyard observing lists](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/backyard-observing-lists/)
- The recommended size is 4–6 targets per evening list. The 110-object Messier list should be "spread out over the course of a year". — [Astronomy.com – Backyard observing lists](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/backyard-observing-lists/)
- One club observer organises lists "by the progression of the constellations transiting his site each month". Lists can also be printed sorted by constellation or RA. A forum user reports less success with very long lists. — [NOVAC Lynx page](https://www.novac.com/wp/observing/johns-astro-page/lynx); [IceInSpace forum](https://www.iceinspace.com.au/forum/archive/index.php/t-143562.html)
- NASA rates each month's objects by difficulty and lists the easiest first, separately per hemisphere. — [NASA Hubble Night Sky Challenge – November](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubbles-night-sky-challenge-november)
- SkySafari's "Tonight's Best" uses dusk-to-dawn darkness for the current location and date. — [SkySafari Observing Lists Help](https://support.simulationcurriculum.com/hc/en-us/articles/115015943028-Observing-Lists-Help)

### Inferences (my own derivation from the sidereal-time sources above; verify in code with astronomy-engine)
- **Formula for evening transit:**
  - LST ≈ local mean solar time + 2 h × (months since about 22 Sept).
  - At 21:00 local mean time (LMT) on the 15th of month m (1 = Jan), the meridian RA is about **RA ≈ (2h × m + 1.5h) mod 24h**.
  - Examples: Jan ≈ 3.5–4.5h, Mar ≈ 8.5h, May ≈ 12.5h, Jul ≈ 16.5h, Sep ≈ 20.5h, Nov ≈ 0.5h.
  - A check using GMST at 0h UT on 15 Jan (about 7.6h) gives 4.6h at 21:00, so allow about ±0.5h of slack.
  - For 22:00, add 1h.
- **The brief's rule "RA ≈ 2h × (month − 3)" does not give the evening sky.** It gives roughly the Sun's RA: 0h at the March equinox, rising 2h per month. Objects near that RA are behind the Sun that month. Evening transit at about 21h is about 9h of RA east of the Sun. For the 22h convention, RA ≈ 2h × (month − 3) + 10h.
- **Clock time vs mean solar time:** the rule uses local mean solar time, not clock time.
  - Convert with clock time − timezone offset + longitude/15°, and subtract 1h under daylight saving time (DST).
  - For Valencia (about 0.4°W, CET/CEST), 21:00 clock time is about 19:58 LMT in winter and about 18:58 LMT in summer. That puts the "evening" meridian about 1–2h of RA earlier than the naive rule.
  - An app should compute with the real clock, the location and astronomy-engine sidereal time rather than the rule of thumb.
- **Twilight:** at 40–50°N in June, astronomical darkness starts around 23:00–00:30 local clock time, or never at about 49°N and above.
  - A fixed 21:00 "evening" is then still twilight. Summer month lists should be built for the start of real darkness (Sun below −12° or −18°), not a fixed clock time.
  - deepskycorner's "astronomical night" approach handles this, but it fails above about 48.5°N in midsummer, where a nautical-darkness fallback is needed.
- **Latitude:** an object belongs in a month list only if, at the chosen evening time, it is high enough at the user's latitude. A reasonable threshold is ≥ 25–30° for faint deep-sky objects, and ≥ 15° in practice for bright ones; the 5° used by deepskycorner is too lenient for a city sky.
  - Example: C65 (Sculptor Galaxy, Dec −25°) culminates at only about 25° from Valencia (39.5°N).
  - NASA handles latitude coarsely with two hemisphere lists. An app can do it exactly.
- **Usefulness:** a "Tonight" list is what most users act on (SkySafari, SkyTools). Month and season lists support planning ahead and browsing, while RA or constellation order supports an efficient session (marathon logic).

### Gaps
- I found no authoritative published statement such as "guides use 21:00 or 22:00 for transit". The evening-transit convention is inferred from the IGN "two hours later" note and the sidereal-time rule. The RASC Observer's Handbook and S&T how-to text on this convention were not accessible.
- I found no survey data on whether amateurs prefer month, season, constellation or tonight lists. The evidence is anecdotal: forum posts and magazine advice.

## Public-domain or permissive data for monthly lists, versus computing from RA and latitude, and recommended app structure

### Takeaway
I found no CC0 or CC-BY dataset that defines month-to-object lists, apart from deepskycorner.ch (CC BY 4.0 website, computed for the equator by default) and NASA's Hubble challenge (likely public domain, but only about 9 objects per month). Computing the month assignment from each object's RA and Dec, the observer's latitude and longitude, and a darkness-based evening window is legally clean, more accurate, and fits what AstroTurboMaxiSolver already computes. It is the better approach.

### Cited Findings
- deepskycorner.ch content is under CC BY 4.0 unless otherwise noted. Its lists are computed by a rule: ≥5° altitude during astronomical night at a set latitude. — [Deep Sky Corner](https://deepskycorner.ch/objects.en.php/month/1)
- NASA media are generally not copyrighted, but NASA must not appear to endorse a product, and its logos are protected. — [NASA media guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)
- SkyMaps.com forbids electronic or commercial use without permission. — [SkyMaps.com Terms](https://skymaps.com/terms.html)
- In-The-Sky.org content is © Dominic Ford, and redistribution requires credit. The full terms are unverified. — [About In-The-Sky.org](https://in-the-sky.org/about.php)
- The month rule: the meridian RA advances about 2h per month at a fixed clock time. — [PhysicsForums](https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/how-can-i-estimate-local-sidereal-time-in-my-head.636728); [IGN SkyAtlas](https://www.ign.es/web/resources/acercaDe/libDigPub/SkyAtlas2023_eng.pdf)

### Inferences (recommendation for AstroTurboMaxiSolver)
- **Compute; don't copy.** For each catalogue object, AstroTurboMaxiSolver already has J2000 RA/Dec, and astronomy-engine gives sidereal time and horizon coordinates. A suggested definition: an object is "in month M" if, on the 15th of M at the observer's location, its altitude at the **start of astronomical darkness** (Sun at −18°) or at 21:00 clock time, whichever is later, is ≥ a threshold, and it is within about ±2h of hour angle of the meridian.
  - The threshold would be about 30° by default, or about 20° for bright objects.
  - The hour-angle condition favours objects "best placed" rather than merely up.
  - A simpler variant is to assign each object to the month in which it transits nearest the start of darkness.
  - This gives a single primary month per object, matching how printed guides present them. It is latitude- and DST-correct automatically, and southern-hemisphere users get correct lists for free.
- **Seasons** follow as groupings of three months: Dec–Feb, Mar–May, Jun–Aug, Sep–Nov, flipped in name for the southern hemisphere. This stays compatible with the RASC Finest NGC season split the app already ships. Note that RASC seasons are written for mid-northern latitudes and evening skies.
- **UI structure suggested by the evidence:**
  - "Tonight" as the default, computed live.
  - "By month", with 12 tabs, the current month preselected, and the hemisphere and latitude applied automatically.
  - Within a month, sort easiest or brightest first (as NASA does) or by RA or constellation for an efficient route.
  - Keep each month list short by default: the top ~10 by magnitude or surface brightness for the user's Bortle class, with "show all".
- **Optional curated seed:** if a hand-picked "showpiece of the month" is wanted, the app's own pick, or one derived from the NASA Hubble monthly targets with neutral wording and no NASA logo, is the lowest-risk option. Confirm the Astronomical League's position before shipping it. Not legal advice.
- **Special months:** a Messier-marathon mode around the March/April new moon is a natural feature, since it orders by RA and needs no third-party data.

### Gaps
- No CC0 or CC-BY machine-readable "objects by month" dataset was found. deepskycorner.ch is CC BY 4.0, but there was no bulk download, and its default latitude of 0° makes it unsuitable as-is.
- The best altitude threshold and evening-time convention for a city sky (about 17.5 mag/arcsec², Valencia) are not established by any source. They should be tuned against real observing or the app's own limiting-magnitude model.
